## What makes a persona qualified

A qualified persona is specific enough that two different people handed the same persona would walk the same store the same way — same entry search, same filters, same objections. "A busy professional" fails this test. "An SMB owner with eight employees, in January, shopping for tax software because their accountant just raised prices" passes it.

The moment matters as much as the person. January changes what the tax-software shopper tolerates; early August changes what the back-to-school parent will pay for shipping speed.

Specificity has a ceiling. The persona must stand for a buyer segment with enough scale that fixing their path moves the business. A persona so narrow that only a handful of real buyers match it produces findings nobody should act on. And the persona is always the product's targeted buyer — derive it from what the product costs, how it positions itself, and who its category serves. When the target buyer is ambiguous, ask the user before walking.

The persona is also always already in market: the trigger put them there. The walk never tests whether demand can be created; it tests whether existing demand finds the product and buys.

## The five fields

Write all five down before walking:

1. **Who.** Role, life context, one humanizing detail that shapes behavior (shops on phone during lunch, hates phone calls, has bought this category before or never has).
2. **The trigger.** Why they are shopping this week and what happens if they don't buy. Deadlines, seasons, breakage, a price increase, a new mandate.
3. **The frame of reference.** What they use today and which alternatives they have already heard of. A switcher compares against what they have; a first-time buyer compares against doing nothing.
4. **The money.** Budget, who approves it, and the math they run. A subscription buyer runs monthly margin math. A consumer runs "is this worth $X to me." A gift buyer runs "will this look like $X."
5. **The words.** Two to four exact phrases they would type into Google, Amazon, or the app store. These become the walk's entry points, so make them the words a real person uses, which are rarely the vendor's words.

## The three tests

Run all three before walking:

1. **The real-person test.** From inside the persona, answer: what is the first thing they type into a search box? What makes them close the tab within ten seconds? What proof do they need before paying? If any answer comes out generic, add context until each answer is obvious.
2. **The scale test.** Does this persona stand for enough buyers that clearing their path changes the numbers? If only a sliver of the market matches the persona, widen it — drop the details that narrow without changing behavior, keep the ones that drive the walk.
3. **The alignment test.** Is this who the product is actually built and priced for? A $30 impulse product and a $500/mo subscription have different buyers running different math. Walking a store as a buyer the product never targeted produces confident findings that should all be ignored.

## How many personas

One primary persona per walk. Add a second only when:

- The buyer and the user differ (a parent buys, a kid uses; an owner buys, the floor staff uses), or
- Two segments walk genuinely different paths (the marketplace shopper and the direct-site shopper for the same product).

Never more than three. Past that the walk loses the perspective shift that makes it useful.

## Lean-back and lean-forward, defined

**Lean-back** is low engagement: on the couch, on the phone, half-attention. The persona skims, gives each screen about three seconds, and taps only what pulls them. This mode surfaces: whether the hero communicates at a glance, whether the thumbnail wins its grid, where the scroll dies, whether anything invites a tap.

**Lean-forward** is high engagement: comparison shopping with money about to move. The persona reads the FAQ, reads the worst review, checks the shipping policy, does the price math, opens a competitor in a second tab. This mode surfaces: unanswered objections, pricing gaps, weak or stale proof, spec omissions, inconsistencies between pages.

Run lean-back first on every channel — first impressions can only happen once — then lean-forward.

## Persona library (calibration examples)

These show the level of specificity to aim for — real enough to be one person, broad enough to be a segment. They are calibration, never a menu: always derive the actual persona from the product being walked and its targeted buyer.

- **The January tax-software shopper.** Owns an eight-person services business. Trigger: accountant raised prices and filing season is eight weeks out. Frame: uses spreadsheets plus the accountant; has heard of the two big brands. Money: $500–1,500/yr, self-approved, runs "hours saved times my rate." Words: "small business tax software," "[big brand] alternative."
- **The back-to-school parent, early August.** Two kids, shops on phone during breaks. Trigger: school starts in three weeks. Frame: last year's brands, whatever survived the wash. Money: ~$400 total, wants free shipping and easy returns. Words: "boys uniform pants," "kids backpacks sale."
- **The operator buying a monthly-priced tool.** Runs a small business, knows their margins cold, converts any subscription price into units-per-month math while reading the pricing page. Trigger: one metric has gone flat and a peer mentioned a fix. Frame: has tried tools in this category before and churned off at least one. Money: needs the math to work at their margin, self-approved under a threshold. Words: "[platform] app [outcome]," "best [category] software for small business." Reads reviews before ever booking a demo.
- **The December gift buyer.** Shopping a category they know nothing about, three weeks before the holiday. Frame: none — proxy signals carry everything: review count, badges, returns policy, delivery promise. Money: fixed gift budget; will pay more to remove risk. Words: "gifts for [recipient] who likes [thing]."
- **The switcher.** Pays a competitor today; shopping because of one specific recurring annoyance. Compares every claim against what they already have, and knows the category's vocabulary. The walk must answer "why is this better than what I've got" without the persona having to ask.
